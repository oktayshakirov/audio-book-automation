# The short-audiobook format

A format reverse-engineered from a self-published title that works, then used
to build one. Nothing here is theory.

## The reference

*Regulate Your F\*cking Nervous System* (Marieme Seck, 11 March 2026). **1 hour
8 minutes, 13 tracks, $3.99 on Spotify, 4.5 stars from 272 ratings.** The
author has a sibling title in the same mould, so it is a repeatable series
format rather than a one-off.

The important observation is the length. **68 minutes, not 8 hours.** This is
the short, cheap, fast-turnaround audiobook, and it is a different product
from a conventional one. Roughly 10,000 words.

## The rules underneath it

**Chapters run 3 to 7 minutes**, roughly 500 to 900 words. Nothing is allowed
to run long.

**The practice chapter is the deliberate outlier**, at 8 minutes. It is the
one people replay, and the one to render first when auditioning a voice.

**Open on a concrete scene, not a thesis.** The reference book's first chapter
is a frog heart in Graz in 1921. Find the equivalent: one real, odd, specific
event that reframes the subject. A famous name alone is not enough - the scene
has to do argumentative work the rest of the book depends on.

**Three short punchy chapters in the middle** naming things the reader has
blamed or been blamed for. These carry the attitude and are the most
shareable.

**Every chapter ends by handing over to the next one.** This is most of why an
hour holds together.

**Chapter titles are second-person accusations with full stops**, or a
concrete physical oddity. Never "Chapter 4: Cortisol."

**Title formula:** an imperative, profanity, and the subject, then a
no-nonsense subtitle in the shape *(And How to Finally ...)*.

## Writing for the ear

1. **No bullet lists, no tables, no "see the table in chapter two."** A
   listener cannot scan back. Naming an earlier chapter as a landmark is fine
   and holds the hour together; sending them back to look something up is not.
2. **Second person, present tense, short declaratives.** Full stops are beats.
3. **Hammer lines get their own paragraph.** That is what the beat rule in
   `pacing.md` keys off, so a one-line paragraph is a performance instruction.
4. **A quotation always gets its own paragraph**, so it inherits a gap. There
   is no reliable way to detect a quote from text alone.
5. **Numbers as words.** "Ninety four percent", not "94%". "Three a.m.", not
   "3AM".
6. **Open on a scene or a flat assertion.** Never "In this chapter we will."
7. **Profanity is seasoning.** It carries the title and lands maybe twice a
   chapter. Wall-to-wall swearing reads as a gimmick.
8. **No em dashes, en dashes or double hyphens, and no curly quotes.** House
   style, and it keeps the synthesiser from stumbling.

## Budgeting a book

At roughly 190 wpm spoken plus two to three minutes of designed pause, 10,000
to 10,500 words lands near 60 minutes. Write the per-track word budget first
and check it as you draft; it is far cheaper than cutting later.

**The only trustworthy runtime is a full render.** Word count times an assumed
wpm is a proxy, and the assumption can be wrong by 30%.
